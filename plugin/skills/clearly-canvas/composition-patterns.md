# Composition choices by deliverable

Numbers below are useful starting ranges in canvas pixels, not fixed standards. Scale
them for the actual viewing distance, content and brand. Never impose a universal
“premium” style on every brief.

## Choose structure by the content

| Content asks for | A structure worth trying | What makes it specific |
|---|---|---|
| One strong claim | Large statement and one piece of evidence | Size contrast and the evidence's visual form |
| A product or object | Deliberately cropped focal image with quiet annotations | Crop, lighting, material and scale |
| Before/after or alternatives | Matched comparison fields, possibly 50/50 | Shared scale, baseline and meaningful differences |
| A story over time | Sequence, timeline or repeated visual motif | Change across panels, controlled rhythm |
| Many related facts | Editorial grid or carefully structured table | Grouping, direct labels, alignment and legibility |
| A process/system | Spatial diagram with meaningful relationships | Direction, proximity, grouping and labeled connectors |

Sketch visual mass before detail. Locate the primary read, supporting read and quiet
space. Use asymmetry for tension when it helps, and symmetry for comparison or calm.
Empty space should reinforce grouping and emphasis; excessive empty space can weaken
information just as crowding can.

## Poster, cover or campaign

For 1080×1350, try 64–96 margins, 96–180 display, 24–36 supporting type, 18–22 detail.
View a 150–250px-wide thumbnail to judge the primary read, then inspect the full image.
The idea might be typographic, photographic, illustrative or mixed. Make the central
visual recognisable and relevant. A repeated abstract ornament is not automatically
a brand asset.

A campaign shares a typographic voice, colour logic, grid and image treatment. Vary
the focal scale or arrangement to suit each message. Design other aspect ratios as
adaptations, moving hierarchy and crop; do not stretch or blindly crop the first board.
Check the target platform's current overlays/safe zones when final publication matters.

## Presentation

For 1920×1080, try 96–120 margins, 64–96 headlines, 28–36 main text and 20–24 labels.
Write the argument as the title. Choose evidence the audience can grasp at presentation
distance. Move supporting detail into notes or a document when it obscures the argument.

Plan the whole sequence: opening promise, evidence, explanation and conclusion. Vary
the visual function: opening image, decisive statistic, diagram, comparison, detail,
closing. Keep title baselines, folios and text roles consistent. Do not force an equal
number of cards onto every slide. Review a contact sheet for rhythm and each slide for fit.

## Page or app mockup

For 1440 desktop, try 80–112 outer margins, 64–88 hero headline and 18–24 supporting
text. For 390 mobile, try 20–24 margins, 36–48 headline and 16–18 body, then measure.
These values are starting points, not a substitute for testing actual copy.

Design a complete reading path: navigation, proposition, evidence, next action and
relevant follow-up content. Use an actual product visual where the page claims product
value. Distinguish interface controls from decorative editorial elements. Include useful
empty/error/loading states when they belong to the requested UI.

Mobile needs its own content order, wrapping, crop and touch affordances. A static
desktop artboard scaled down is not a mobile design. If delivering implementation,
verify keyboard access and responsive behavior in the real browser as well.

## Editorial and information design

Begin with reading order and density. Align related labels and values, give numbers
a consistent unit/precision, and use direct chart annotations. Let the amount of content
determine the number of columns. Body measure often works around 45–75 characters;
captions and labels can be shorter. Check actual line breaks rather than calculating
character count alone.

Use rules and backgrounds where they clarify grouping. Remove boxes that merely frame
every paragraph. A chart, map or diagram should carry information, with honest scales
and visible sources when supplied. Never invent evidence to make a layout look complete.

## A useful critique names the fix

Weak: “Make it more polished.”

Useful: “At thumbnail size the orange badge wins before the title. Reduce its area and
move it beside the caption. The product image needs a larger crop to explain the claim.
The second paragraph wraps into the footer; shorten its measure only after reserving
more vertical space, or edit the copy.”

Judge the artifact against its audience and brief. A quiet museum catalogue, dense
operations dashboard and vivid music poster should not converge on the same aesthetic.
