Books a studio designer works from, each reduced to one rule you can act on. The in-app

## How to use it

- In Research, pick two or three references whose lens fits the brief: a print piece or poster → grid, type, print; an identity → brand; a chart or report → information; a screen or flow → interface. Write what each one changes in this design.
- In Direction, name the reference behind each direction's idea in one line ("a Müller-Brockmann modular grid, type as the image after Scher").
- In Review, critique against the same rules you chose.
- These are paraphrased principles, not quotations. Never invent a quote or page number; cite author and title only.

## Method — deciding before drawing

- Massimo Vignelli, *The Vignelli Canon* (2010): Answer semantics (what it means, to whom), syntactics (its visual grammar) and pragmatics (will they get it) before placing a node; few typefaces, a grid, discipline.
- Michael Bierut, *How to* (2015): Present brief → idea → trade-offs; the idea comes out of the problem, and the rationale is part of the deliverable.
- Paul Rand, *Thoughts on Design* (1947): Form and content are one: simplify until the idea is legible, and allow wit — play is a way of making it memorable.
- Bruno Munari, *Design as Art* (1966): Solve the problem with the simplest means; decoration that solves nothing is noise.
- Karl Gerstner, *Designing Programmes* (1964): Design the rules, not one layout: define the variables (size, position, colour) and generate the set from them.
- Dieter Rams, *Less but Better* (1995): As little design as possible: every element must earn its place by use; remove what does not.
- Alan Fletcher, *The Art of Looking Sideways* (2001): Look for the idea sideways — a visual pun or found connection beats a literal illustration of the brief.

## Grid and layout

- Josef Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* (1981): Construct margins, columns, gutter and baseline first; every element lands on the grid; break it once, on purpose.
- Timothy Samara, *Making and Breaking the Grid* (2002): Pick the grid for the content (manuscript, column, modular, hierarchical); a deliberate break needs a grid to break.
- Kimberly Elam, *Geometry of Design* (2001): Place the focal point on a proportional line (golden section, root rectangles, thirds), not at dead centre.
- Beth Tondreau, *Layout Essentials* (2009): Grids by job: a single column for reading, multi-column for mixed content, modular for catalogues and dashboards.

## Typography

- Robert Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style* (1992): Measure 45–75 characters, leading to suit the measure, honour the content: type exists to be read.
- Ellen Lupton, *Thinking with Type* (2004): Build hierarchy by changing one variable at a time — size, weight, colour or space — and track capitals open.
- Jan Tschichold, *The New Typography* (1928): Asymmetric, functional layout: hierarchy by size, weight and position, never ornament.
- Emil Ruder, *Typographie* (1967): White space is active form: the counter, the gap and the margin are composed like the type itself.
- Jost Hochuli, *Detail in Typography* (1987): Letter, word and line spacing decide readability: tighten display type, keep word spaces even, open the leading for long lines.
- Erik Spiekermann and E. M. Ginger, *Stop Stealing Sheep & Find Out How Type Works* (1993): A typeface is a voice: choose it for the message and audience, then set it with care before anything else.
- Ellen Lupton, *Type on Screen* (2014): On screen, type is motion and interaction too: sizes, scales and states are a system, not one setting.

## Print and editorial production

- Jan Tschichold, *The Form of the Book*: Classical page proportions: inner margin smallest, foot margin largest; the text block sits high and toward the spine.
- Richard Hendel, *On Book Design* (1998): A book design serves the reading; decide trim, margins, type and running heads together, and stay invisible.
- Andrew Haslam, *Book Design* (2006): Plan the whole object — format, grid, pagination, binding — before designing a spread.
- Jan V. White, *Editing by Design* (1974): Editorial layout starts from what the story says; headline, picture and caption are one unit.
- International Paper, *Pocket Pal*: Production rules: CMYK for press, 300 dpi images at final size, 3 mm (0.125 in) bleed, a safe area inside the trim.
- Alan Pipes, *Production for Graphic Designers*: Specify for the press: paper stock, ink coverage, rich black for large areas only, overprint and trap small type.

## Colour

- Josef Albers, *Interaction of Color* (1963): Colour is relative: judge it beside its neighbours on the real ground, never as a swatch alone.
- Johannes Itten, *The Art of Color* (1961): Choose the one contrast that leads (light–dark, warm–cool, complementary, saturation, extent) and let the rest support it.

## Graphic form and visual perception

- Armin Hofmann, *Graphic Design Manual* (1965): Build with elementary contrasts — point, line, plane, black and white, large and small — before colour.
- Ellen Lupton and Jennifer Cole Phillips, *Graphic Design: The New Basics* (2008): Point, line, plane, rhythm, scale, texture, figure/ground and framing are variables; change one deliberately.
- Wucius Wong, *Principles of Two-Dimensional Design* (1972): Repetition, gradation and radiation give a field structure; an anomaly in that structure is the focal point.
- Rudolf Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception* (1954): Visual weight balances on the frame's hidden axes; size, value, isolation and position all add weight.
- Robin Williams, *The Non-Designer's Design Book* (1994): Contrast, repetition, alignment, proximity: if two things differ, make them clearly different.
- Alex W. White, *The Elements of Graphic Design* (2002): Treat space as an element: emptiness gives the content its shape and its importance.
- Kenya Hara, *Designing Design* (2007): Emptiness invites the viewer in: leave room for the reader's imagination instead of filling the frame.
- Paula Scher, *Make It Bigger* (2002): Type can be the image: scale and crop a word until it carries the identity on its own.
- Philip B. Meggs, *A History of Graphic Design* (1983): Know the lineage of a style so a reference is chosen, not a pastiche of the first thing found.
- Adrian Frutiger, *Signs and Symbols*: A sign reads by its simplest form; test a mark small, at a distance and in one colour.

## Brand and identity

- Alina Wheeler, *Designing Brand Identity* (2003): Identity is a process — research, strategy, design, touchpoints, asset management; design the touchpoints, not just the logo.
- Marty Neumeier, *The Brand Gap* (2003): A brand is the customer's gut feeling; close the gap between strategy and the look by testing what people actually perceive.
- Marty Neumeier, *Zag* (2006): When everyone zigs, zag: state what makes this the only one, then design to that difference.
- Wally Olins, *On Brand* (2003): A brand shows in product, environment, communication and behaviour; keep all four saying the same thing.
- Byron Sharp, *How Brands Grow* (2010): Growth comes from being easy to notice and buy: protect distinctive assets and stay consistent.
- Jenni Romaniuk, *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* (2018): An asset (colour, shape, character) works once it is famous and unique to the brand; use it everywhere, change it rarely.
- Tom Geismar, Ivan Chermayeff and Sagi Haviv, *Identify* (2011): A mark must be appropriate, distinctive and simple; it earns meaning through use over time.
- David Airey, *Logo Design Love* (2009): Test a logo at 16 px, in one colour and in reverse; if it needs detail or colour to survive, simplify it.
- Danne & Blackburn, *NASA Graphics Standards Manual* (1975): A standards manual fixes the mark, clear space, colours, type, grid and applications so anyone can apply the system.
- Unimark International (Vignelli, Noorda), *New York City Transit Authority Graphics Standards Manual* (1970): A system scales by rules: one typeface, fixed modules, colour coding and consistent placement.

## Information design

- Edward R. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983): Maximise data-ink: remove chart junk, label directly, show the comparison; use small multiples for change.
- Edward R. Tufte, *Envisioning Information* (1990): Layer and separate: quiet the grid and background so the data reads first.
- Otto Neurath, *International Picture Language* (1936): Isotype: count with repeated identical pictograms, never by enlarging one.

## Interface and product design

- Don Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things* (1988): Make the action visible: signifiers, feedback, mapping and constraints; a control must say how it is used.
- Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think* (2000): People scan, they do not read: obvious hierarchy, conventions, one clear next action per page.
- Adam Wathan and Steve Schoger, *Refactoring UI* (2018): Hierarchy by weight and colour before size, de-emphasise secondary text, use a spacing and shadow scale.
- Jenifer Tidwell, *Designing Interfaces* (2005): Use the known pattern for the job (navigation, forms, lists, dashboards) before inventing one.
- William Lidwell, Kritina Holden and Jill Butler, *Universal Principles of Design* (2003): Check the design against named principles (Fitts, Hick, proximity, progressive disclosure) and say which one a choice serves.
